# Story structures that connect scripture to modern life

Read OpenMontage `skills/creative/storytelling.md`, `short-form.md` and `long-form.md` for general craft; this file adds what is specific to SutraVerse.

## The four questions every video answers
1. **What does the teaching actually say?** (verified, in context)
2. **Why does it matter to me today?** (a specific, believable modern situation)
3. **What do people get wrong about it?** (the misunderstanding beat — often the most shareable insight)
4. **What do I do differently tomorrow?** (one to three concrete actions)

## Short-form (45–60 s) — flexible spine
| Beat | Time | Job | Example (anger, Gita 2.62–63) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | 0–3 s | A recognisable moment or a surprising claim; on-screen question | "Someone took credit for your work… and it replayed all day?" |
| Problem | 3–10 s | Name the modern pain precisely | "Anger feels sudden. It isn't." |
| Connection | 10–15 s | Who said what, where (reference chip) | Krishna to Arjuna, Gita ch. 2 |
| Teaching | 15–35 s | The idea, visualised (chain, contrast, story beat) | dwelling → attachment → desire → anger → delusion → … |
| Insight / misunderstanding | 35–42 s | The turn | "Anger is not the first link — dwelling is." |
| Application | 42–52 s | 1–3 concrete actions in modern terms | stop the replay; wait before replying; address the issue, not the ego |
| Close + CTA | 52–60 s | One memorable line, brand CTA, end card | "Break the chain where it begins." |

## Long-form (6–10 min) — chaptered
Cold open (real situation or question, 30–45 s; the hook lands in the first 3 s) → short brand sting scene → context (who/where/why in the text) → the story or teaching in scenes → deeper meaning (with commentary attributed) → 2–3 modern examples from different life areas (work, family, self) → common misunderstandings → practical framework → recap in one line → reflective close + CTA. Add chapter markers in `publish.md` from `timing.json` scene starts. Introduce a fresh insight at least every 60–90 s.

## Hooks — patterns, not formulas
- Recognition: a precise, everyday moment ("You replied to that message in anger. Again.")
- Reversal: "Karna wasn't destroyed by Arjuna. He was destroyed by a promise."
- Question with stakes: "Why did Krishna tell Arjuna to fight — and you to stop?"
- Misconception: "'Do your duty without expecting results' doesn't mean what you think."
Never: fake urgency, false claims about the text, "You won't believe…", fear-mongering about afterlife, or the same hook shape twice in a row in a series.

## Modern examples
Specific and Indian: a Bengaluru stand-up meeting, a family WhatsApp group, a Diwali argument about marriage, EMI stress, a sibling property dispute, Instagram comparison at 1 a.m., a board-exam result. Avoid generic Western stock scenarios. Keep examples respectful and non-judgemental; show choices, not villains.

## Tone guardrails
No preaching, no guru voice, no simplistic moral verdicts, no supernatural exaggeration presented as fact, no invented history. Ambiguous characters (Karna, Bhishma, Draupadi, Ravana) deserve nuance — present the tension.

## CTAs
Natural and occasional: invite reflection ("Which link do you usually miss?"), sharing ("Send this to the friend who replies too fast"), or following. The end card carries the brand CTA; don't add a second hard sell in the narration.
